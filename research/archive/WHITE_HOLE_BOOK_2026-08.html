01 / The water

Your six miles, and how long the water takes to find you

River miles below Bull Shoals Dam, derived from your own pinned GPS points and scaled so the White Hole lands on the travel model's calibrated 7.0. Cranor's Island is an estimate; there is no GPS chain below the White Hole. The arrival times come from the repo's travel model across one to five units, so each is a range — that is your safety clock, and it is the single most useful number on this page.

RM 0 Bull Shoals Dam Eight turbines, ~3,300 cfs each. No fishing within 100 yards, ever.
RM 1.3–3 State Park & Dew Eddy Shoal Catch-and-release, artificials only, barbless, no bait — Feb 1 to Oct 31. Your April and October trips both land inside that window, so William can't fish bait up here. Worth an hour of your own time at low light in October; it closes entirely on Nov 1.
RM 4.1 Gaston's — your upstream limit Deep hole-and-weed-bed water. This is the more forgiving prop run from White Hole: deeper, no shoal to cross. Rise arrives 1.3–1.9 hr
RM ~6 Stetson's Resort Private dock. Locals list it alongside White Hole as the place to get a boat to the bank in a hurry if water comes up while you're between the two.
RM 7.0 White Hole The "White Hole" is a deep hole in the river, not a place name — and the resort sits at its upstream lip. Which means the deep water starts at the ramp and runs downstream from it. There are also weed beds a short run upstream of the ramp that hold rising rainbows and browns, and they fish even at four and five units. Those two features are your home water. Rise arrives 2.2–3.3 hr
RM ~9 Cranor's White River Lodge On the Denton Ferry side, inside of the bend — confirmed as your landmark. It's the only riverside lodge with a dock between White Hole and Wildcat.
RM ~9.5 Cranor's Island — your downstream limit The island right below Cranor's White River Lodge, and your name for it. Fish both sides; the deepest water is on the far side. Locals fish bigger nymphs here, #14 up to #8. It is also the spot on this reach most likely to strand somebody — water comes up very fast and crossings go impassable in minutes. The research brief called this the Narrows; that name found no corroboration and was dropped. Rise arrives 3.0–4.5 hr
RM 11–12 Wildcat Shoals Past your usual turnaround, and described locally as "almost impassable." If you ever do go, the shoal is downstream and around the bend from the ramp, not at it.
The trick this reach is built for

Water falls out at roughly river miles ÷ 2 = hours. So after a shutdown, Gaston's clears in about 2½ hours, White Hole in 4, Cranor's Island in 5. Run downstream in the morning to stay in low water longer, and work back upstream in the evening as it drops out. You get two extra hours of low water at Cranor's Island compared to Gaston's, for free.

There is no warning siren downstream

There's a horn at the dam and it is inaudible from anywhere you'll be. Assume no warning. Tie off bow upstream, tie high with slack in the rope, keep a sharp fixed-blade knife within arm's reach, and re-tend the line every 20–30 minutes once you know water is coming. Most of the drownings on this river are people who threw an anchor out during generation — so the way you and William already fish it is the safe way.

02 / Decide in thirty seconds

Read the dam, then pick your play

Tap what's running. Everything below it changes — sinker weight for a boat that isn't moving, what bait goes on, how you present it, and whether you can get out and wade.

Minimum flow · ~700–850 cfs · the river is a big cold spring creek
Where the numbers actually live

There is no USGS discharge gauge anywhere in your reach. The three USGS sites below the dam log water temperature and dissolved oxygen only — no flow, no stage. Flow comes from the Corps and from SWPA instead.

The SWPA schedule posts megawatts by hour-ending, so "1400" means 1:00–2:00 pm. 7 MW = minimum flow. Divide MW by ~49 for units; multiply by ~67.5 for cfs. The USACE tabular page runs 2–3 hours behind, so use it for what happened and the SWPA forecast for what's coming.

03 / The rig

The rig you already tie, with the numbers filled in

You're on the right rig — this is the local dimensional spec, now built as the tippet-ring cartridge system: a permanent base that lives on the rod, and a pre-tied hook leader that clinches to a ring and gets swapped as the water moves.

Tying it

  1. Build the base out of 8 lb fluorocarbon

    Size 10 barrel swivel at the top, then about 12 in of butt down to where the dropper loop goes. This piece is permanent — it stays on the rod all week.

  2. Tie the dropper loop and cut one side

    That's the Y. The standing line runs on through the knot uncut and carries on downward — only the cut tag is a branch, and that tag, 6–10 in, is what carries the bell. Built this way a fish pulls against unbroken line through the junction, and the knot's tag, which is the weakest part of a dropper loop, only ever holds lead.

    On snaggy bottom, attach the bell to the tag with a rubber band so it breaks away and costs you a fifteen-cent sinker instead of the rig.

  3. Finish the base with an 8 in stub and a 2 mm tippet ring

    The ring is the hinge the whole system turns on. Everything above it is permanent; everything below it is consumable.

  4. Clinch a cartridge to the ring

    Improved clinch on both sides — a Palomar won't thread 2 mm with a three-foot leader. The cartridge is what picks the species program: 4 lb mono for rainbows, 8 lb fluoro for browns.

    12, 24 or 36 in of cartridge gives a hook leg of 20, 32 or 44 in. Run 44 at minimum flow, 32 around one unit, 20 from two units up, and trim to tune. Swap up a length as the water drops.

  5. Pre-tie the wallet at home

    Wound on a foam disc: a few of each length in both line classes, plus a couple of #1 sculpin leaders. Changing the whole presentation on the water then costs one knot.

Check your hooks before you leave home

Across the whole Bull Shoals tailwater, bait fishing is single-hooking-point only. If either of you has been putting PowerBait on a treble down there, that's not legal. Trebles are fine on lures outside the catch-and-release zones — just not with bait, scent, or dough on them.

The adjustment nobody tells you

Every published weight chart for the White is a drift chart — sized so the sinker ticks bottom while the boat moves with the water. You aren't moving. Your sinker has to resist the full velocity of the river, so run roughly double the drift weight. The calibration is: cast quartering upstream, let it settle. It should hold, then slip a few inches and re-grab when you lift the tip. Never moves = too heavy, you'll miss bites and snag. Never stops = too light.

04 / Bait

If you want bigger fish, PowerBait is the wrong end of the list

PowerBait's strength is fresh stockers, and the White has very few of those right now — the Norfork hatchery lost most of its stock in late 2025 and the reach above Norfork Access hasn't seen a meaningful stocking since. What's left is a higher proportion of holdovers and wild-behaving fish eating natural food, with more forage per predator. Your odds on a genuinely big fish are arguably better than normal. Your odds on a dozen naïve rainbows are worse. Fish accordingly.

BaitHow

2Soft-shell crawdad or crawdad tailNamed in report after report as the top natural bait for browns, and badly under-fished. Most relevant in October, when the water is warmest and crawfish are active.

Get the bait off the bottom — it fixes two problems at once

A floating bait riding 6–18 inches up snags far less and sits where the fish are looking. Three ways, all cheap: inflate the crawler with a worm blower, thread a mini marshmallow up the shank ahead of the bait (documented local practice, not folklore), or use floating Mice Tails / Power Eggs. Use mono, not fluoro, on the hook leg — fluoro sinks and kills the lift.

Rig a Mice Tail through the center of the worm and out about half an inch behind the head, so it floats horizontally. Hooked at the head it hangs vertically, which is wrong.

05 / Presentation

Four ways to cover water from a boat that can't move

You can't reposition, so the bait has to. Work them in this order from every spot you tie up.

  1. Quartering upstream, then let it hang

    Cast 45° up, let it sink on slack, follow it with the rod tip as it comes past, then let it come tight below the boat and leave it there. A lot of takes come at the dangle, because that's where the bait has sat longest in front of a fish.

  2. Straight downstream — the high-water play

    This is guide Carl Meeks' anchored technique, and it maps exactly onto your setup: two whole night crawlers threaded on a #6 with the tails left dangling, cast downstream and left in the current. The flow holds the leader straight and lifts the bait in the column. Don't yank it back when it starts to rise.

  3. Walk the rig down

    The one that turns a fixed boat into a hundred-yard drift. With the rig on bottom below you: lift the tip to unweight the sinker, feed three to six feet of line, let it re-settle. Repeat. New bottom every cycle, no recast. Grid it — near seam first, then a rod length out, then the far seam.

  4. The float rig — and you already own all of it

    At minimum flow through about two units, this is the closest thing to drifting without drifting, and William never has to know. Set the float so the bait rides 1–3 ft off bottom, cast up, and let it run 40–80 feet downstream on a controlled feed, then reel back and repeat. The slip float, the bobber stops and the orange beads in your rigging box plus a 1/16 oz panfish head and a 2" white grub is the local jig-under-float rig. Above two units, go back to the bottom rig.

Where to tie the boat

Meeks' rule, adapted: put the boat in the faster water and fish the slower — or, tied to the bank, sit in the slack and cast to the seam where slow meets fast. Concretely, on your reach:

  • The head of the White Hole, just below the ramp, where the deep water starts.
  • The edge of the weed beds upstream of the White Hole ramp — those beds are where the sowbugs and scuds live and trout sit on the edge picking them off. They fish even at four or five units.
  • Both sides of Cranor's Island, remembering the deepest water is on the far side.
  • The drop-off at the tail of any shoal, where gravel falls into the run. That's the ambush lane, and in October it's where staging browns sit.
  • Undercut banks, root wads, log jams, submerged timber, and the head of every hole.
  • Once generation starts: flooded grass and gravel bars that were dry an hour ago, and the bank edge itself. Fish push there for the dislodged food and the softer current.

Rotate every 45–60 minutes. Untie, drop 100 yards, retie. It costs five minutes and it's the only way you cover the reach.

Rooster Tails from a fixed boat

1/6 oz is the size — it's cited more than any other on this river. 1/8 for low clear water, 1/4 once four units are on. Gold blade with yellow or orange body, silver blade with white or pink, brown skirt for browns, chartreuse/black as the oddball. Blade rule: gold in bright clear water, silver or nickel on cloudy and foggy mornings.

Work the clock face: quartering upstream is the primary — let it sink first, then a steady retrieve with the blade thumping, and expect the hit as it starts to swing across. Then straight across. Then let it swing all the way below the boat and hold it there — the blade turns on flow alone, which is a free presentation only a stationary boat gets. Then feed line in three-to-six-foot increments to walk it down the run.

Never clip a snap swivel straight to the spinner — it throws the blade off. Run 18 inches of fluoro to a small barrel swivel up the line.

A connection worth making

You have fifty Reaction Tackle drop-shot hooks, size #1, red. Size #1 is big for trout generally, but it's right for a sculpin, a crawdad tail or a whole crawler — and a drop-shot hook is built to ride perpendicular off a tag line, which is exactly the geometry of the river rig's long hook leg. Nose-hitch one onto the leg and it does the job it was designed for. One of the better-known guides down there fishes this river on a drop-shot rod for the same reason.

The three real mismatches

  1. Your leader material is all wrong — and this is the one that matters

    20 lb fluoro, 30 lb fluoro and 20 lb coated wire is the pickerel tax. The White is gin clear and 48–56°F, and the rig runs on exactly two spools: 4 lb mono for the rainbow program, 8 lb fluorocarbon for browns. The 8 lb is not a lapse in stealth — you cannot land that program's target on 4 lb around wood, and the base sits 20-odd inches above the bait, so the fish only ever inspects the cartridge. About $12 for both, and nothing else on this page will change your catch rate as much.

  2. No small bait hooks

    A #4 Aberdeen fishes a Mice Tail or a dough ball fine, just oversized for it. #6–#8 light wire is the spec, and it's a three-dollar pack. Optional — the #4s will catch fish.

  3. No 1/6 oz spinner

    1/6 oz is the Rooster Tail size on this river and you don't have one. But your flame/chartreuse ¼ oz Rooster Tail, the Mepps Aglia #3 silver and the Black Fury #3 all sit in the quarter-ounce class — right from two units up — and the 1/16 Beetle Spin covers the bottom end. That brackets the gap closely enough that I wouldn't spend money on it.

Two things to leave in Ossining

The whole topwater tray — Ploppers, poppers, Hula Popper, Crazy Crawler, buzzbaits — plus the spinnerbait, the Silver Minnows, the Senkos and wacky sticks, the football jigs and the wire leader. None of it has a job on this river.

And the method feeders and the jello/panko. Pack bait isn't a White River technique, and chumming is explicitly prohibited in the catch-and-release areas. Leave it home.

One legal catch on the spoons

Tipping a spoon or a spinner with shrimp or a crawdad tail is a named local technique and it works. But the moment bait or scent goes on it, the single-hooking-point rule applies — so swap the treble for a single before you tip anything.

The inventory doesn't list rods and reels. What this water wants is a 6½–7 ft light or medium-light spinning rod, a 1000-size reel with a smooth drag, and 4–6 lb clear or light-green mono on it.

07 / Your two weeks

October and April are almost opposite trips

This is your better window, and it isn't close. Median flow in early October runs about a third of early April. The documented fall pattern is minimum flow from dawn through mid-morning with generation arriving in the afternoon or evening — which is exactly the shape that gives you a low-water morning and then a fishable rise.

  • Warmest water of the year. The release peaks around 53–56°F in early fall, and at minimum flow it picks up another few degrees by the time it reaches Cranor's Island. That is the top of the trout feeding range — fish will actually chase.
  • Browns are pre-spawn and staging. Spawning on this river runs roughly mid-October into November, so early October is genuinely the last clean window: full pre-spawn aggression, no redds to fish over yet. They've shifted hard onto eating fish. Sculpin here run 5–6 inches — don't go small.
  • Where they'll be: the upper ends of holes (one guide's specific observation is oversize browns stacked in five feet of water at the head of a hole), the drop-off at the tail of every shoal, undercut banks and timber.
  • Go fish-imitating. Sculpin, crawdad, big inflated crawlers, jerkbaits, brown-trout-pattern Countdowns. Keep a bottom rig with eggs going for the rainbows in between.
  • Best hours: first light to about ten, and the last two hours of daylight — plus every generation change, whenever it lands.
  • One handling note: dissolved oxygen in the release is at its annual low late summer into early fall. Land them fast and keep them wet.
  • The dam catch-and-release mile is open until Oct 31 and closes entirely on Nov 1. Artificials, barbless, no bait. Skinny pressured water that holds disproportionately big fish — worth a morning of your own time with the 5-weight.

Highest-variance week of your year. April was the higher-flow month in twelve of the last fourteen years, and in the wet ones the Corps runs 16,000–24,000 cfs around the clock with no morning window at all. In dry years it looks like October. Plan the boat, treat wading as a bonus.

  • Coldest water of your two trips, roughly 48–52°F. Fish still eat but they won't move far. Slow, deep, dead-drifted beats fast and reactive, and the bite skews later in the day.
  • Caddis is the marquee event but you're on its front edge. The real emergence has been sliding later into April, it needs flows around 4,000 cfs or less to truly fire, and it works its way upstream from the lower river — so your reach lags Cotter and Rim Shoals. If you get it, the tell is evening swarms after 5 pm.
  • Worms on the rise are a top-three producer. The advice is almost verbatim from the state's own reports: cast live worms near the banks as the water comes up. First hour of a rise, tight to the bank.
  • High-water rig: more weight, longer leader, fish the seams and defined runs in four to five feet along the banks, not the open middle. Flooded grass beds are the target.
  • Overcast and rain are the good days, both for the hatch and for big browns moving shallow to hunt.
  • Don't count on a fresh stocking. Post-hatchery-collapse, nothing meaningful has gone into your reach. Verify with AGFC before you build a day around stockers.
  • Watch Crooked Creek and the Buffalo. When they rise, the Corps cuts generation to protect the towns downstream — which can hand you a surprise low-water window.
08 / Your own rod

What the Recon can do from a boat seat

The good news: a tied-off boat is a fixed position, and the best local outfitter says plainly that swinging streamers "works best wading or from fixed positions." Your constraint is actually your advantage. The 5-weight is enough — every shop on this river names the 9-foot 5 as the standard rod, and its real limits are big flies and wind, not fish size.

  1. Tightline the near seam — start here

    The highest fish-per-hour method available to you and the most seat-friendly thing you can do. Strip the fly line back to the tip, run a long thin leader with a heavy jig point fly and a small dropper 18 inches above, flick it 8–15 feet upstream into the seam and lead it down with the tip high. No casting, no line management, no wind problem. In a breeze it's the only thing that'll work well.

  2. Swing a soft hackle or small streamer

    Shorten the leader to 6–7½ ft, cast across and slightly down, one upstream mend, let it come tight and swing below the boat. Don't strike — let the fish take against the swing. Let it hang and pulse it for six to ten seconds at the dangle. Then cover water by lengthening: 20 ft, 25, 30, 35. You'll fish a forty-foot arc without moving the boat.

  3. Short indicator drift when they're deep

    Cast up and out 20–25 feet, no more — anything longer is un-mendable from a seat. Lead the indicator with the rod tip, then drop the tip and follow it down, and let the swing at the end fish. Stick-on indicators at low water; Airlock or Thingamabobber once you're carrying real weight.

  4. Dry-dropper for prospecting

    October hoppers, April caddis. Fish it into the soft water the boat itself creates — the slack behind the hull, the bank slot inside your tie-off — where drag isn't the problem it is out in the current.

The one purchase worth about fifteen dollars

A fast-sinking VersiLeader or poly-leader, looped onto the tip of your existing floating line, plus 3–4 ft of the same 8 lb fluorocarbon the spin rig runs on. The local shop calls it an "insta-sink-tip" and recommends it as the versatile stand-in for a dedicated sinking line. With that and a weighted #6–10 streamer, swung rather than stripped, the Recon does most of what a 7-weight would. Swinging is what makes a 5-weight adequate for streamers; trying to bomb and strip big flies is what makes it inadequate.

Eight flies and you're covered

Scuds and sowbugs are the primary diet on this river year-round; midges are the most numerous insect. Everything else is seasonal decoration.

  • Sunday Special #14 and #12 with a tungsten bead — the single most-named fly on this water
  • Gray sowbug #14–16 (drop to #16–18 dead low)
  • Ruby Midge #16–18, tungsten bead — the sunny-day, low-water dropper
  • Zebra Midge #16–18, black/nickel and red/nickel
  • San Juan worm, fluorescent pink — high water, and the first hour of every rise
  • Egg, peach or orange — essential in October
  • Olive Woolly Bugger #8–10 — swings beautifully from a tied boat, cheapest effective streamer there is
  • Black Girdle Bug #8–10 — the high-water anchor fly

Add for April: a Tailwater Soft Hackle in caddis green #14 (your swinging fly) and an Elk Hair Caddis #14. Add for October: a #10 hopper.

The tenkara rods — the honest answer

Bring them, use them for an hour, don't build anything around them. The Kurenai 30R and 33R are rated for trout of 4–10 inches with the occasional 11-incher, and the maker explicitly says they're too light for consistent 14-inch fish. The average White River rainbow is at or above that line — the regulations are literally written around a 14-inch rainbow. There's no warranty on those rods either.

Where they genuinely work: true minimum flow only, in the soft shallow edges and tailouts of a shoal, in backwaters and eddies, and — this one is legitimately good — the soft bank slot right beside the tied-off boat, where the 33 reaches twenty-plus feet from a seat with no backcast. Size 2.5 level line, 3–4 ft of 6X, one unweighted or micro-bead fly, no tungsten. Anything with real push puts a belly in that line instantly, you can't get a fly deeper than about eighteen inches, and the valley is windy in both your windows.

If you ever want fixed-line to be a real tool here, the answer is a keiryu rod — stiffer mid-section, handles weight, hooks up at depth — not another tenkara rod. Not a purchase, just a note for the file.

Two rods in one small boat

  • Bow upstream — then both lines trail downstream and can't cross under the hull.
  • William in the stern fishing straight down, line static on the bottom in the boat's own lane. You forward, casting up and across to the opposite side. Different quadrants, never intersect.
  • Roll cast and side-arm, or use an oval/Belgian cast. A 9-foot rod and a beaded nymph at eye level with your brother sitting behind you is a real injury risk. Both of you in glasses.
  • A five-gallon bucket with a couple inches of water in it for the fly line. Best six dollars in the boat — loose line on a jon boat floor finds every cleat and rod butt there is.
  • Don't fish the fly into his bait zone. He's covered it, and you'll foul his line.
  • One rod per angler, attended at all times — so no propping a bait rod in a holder while you cast.
09 / When it drops out

Getting out and wading

Minimum flow is roughly 700–850 cfs, and it sits about 12–16 inches higher than the old pre-2013 low water — places that used to be easy are now marginal. Two units or less is the general wading threshold on this river, but treat that as optimistic in your reach.

  • Cranor's Island — low water only, and stay on the western side. Crossings go impassable fast and this is the likeliest place on your reach to get stranded.
  • White Hole — bank and wade fishing at minimum flow; at four or five units it isn't wadeable. The resort's own guidance: 1–3 generators raises it 1–4 feet, 4–8 generators raises it 4–10 feet and wading is unsafe. Levels can come up several feet inside an hour.
  • The dam catch-and-release mile (a drive, not a boat ride) — shallow, slow, spring-creek water, fishable at one to two units, and technical: 6X, indicator only a foot to eighteen inches above the fly, spooky pressured fish. Open Feb 1 to Oct 31. Artificials, barbless, no bait.
Wading rules for a river with no siren

Do the arithmetic before you get in: miles below the dam ÷ 4 mph = your hours of warning. Set a stick at the waterline and check it every ten minutes — you notice depth, not the wave. Know your exit before you wade and never let the river get between you and it. Never wade more than halfway across. Studded boots, wading staff, belt cinched. The water is 48–56°F and cold shock comes fast.

10 / Rules and numbers

What's legal, and who to call

Verify this one before you go — it's changed recently and sources disagree

As of Feb 1, 2026, following the hatchery collapse: Bull Shoals Dam down to Norfork Access — two rainbow trout under 14 inches per day, every other trout released immediately. That covers your entire reach and makes it effectively a brown-trout catch-and-release fishery. AGFC's own trout page still carries this; the eRegulations site still shows the old five-fish rule. Call AGFC at 833-345-0325 before the trip rather than trusting either page.

  • Fishing license plus a trout permit, 16 and over.
  • One rod per angler, attended at all times.
  • Bait fishing: single hooking point only, across the whole tailwater down to the Hwy 58 bridge at Guion.
  • No fishing within 100 yards of the dam, ever.
  • Bull Shoals catch-and-release area, 100 yards below the dam to the upstream state park boundary: Feb 1–Oct 31, all trout released, artificials or flies only, all hook points barbless, no natural or scented bait, no chumming. Closed entirely Nov 1–Jan 31.
  • Your reach — Gaston's down to Cranor's Island — is below all of that and under general tailwater rules.
